## What is the Doppler Effect
The classical doppler effect is when the source of a wave moves in relation to
its [observer](/pages/reference-frame "Reference Frame"), which changes the
[properties of the wave](/pages/waves "Waves"). Simply, a moving object can make
different waves to a stationary object.

## Understanding the Doppler Effect
The first step to understanding the doppler effect is to understand how waves
move. Waves do inherit any movement from the thing that created them, they just
appear in the point that they were made and ripple out. They are always the same
regardless of whether their source was moving or not. This can be visualised by
the simulation below. You can click to create a wave, no matter how fast your
mouse cursor is moving, the wave appears from the point that you clicked on and
spreads out in all directions at the same rate.

{{< wave-sim >}}

This next simulation has a car that is always making a sound. Drag the car
around. As the car moves, it creates waves that come from the point that the car
was when it made them, the same as when you were clicking the mouse. Notice how
when you drag the car around, the lines of the circles are much closer together
in front of the car, and much further apart behind the car.

{{< car-wave-sim >}}

This is the doppler effect in action. As the car moves, the new waves are being
created closer to the edge of the previous wave, which results in the edges
being much closer on that side. The lines of the circles represent the peaks of
the waves, and so a shorter gap between the lines means the wavelength is
shorter. Because of what we know about properties of waves, this shorter
wavelength leads to a higher frequency. So if you are in front of the car you
will experience a higher frequency wave, which would be a higher pitched sound.
Likewise, if you are behind the car you will experience a lower frequency wave
which would be a lower pitched sound. Imagine the 2D graph of the wave being
stretched out or squashed depending on whether you are in front of or behind the
source of the wave when it is moving

The doppler effect explains why when a car drives past you, you can hear the
noise getting higher pitched, and then lower pitched in the moment that it
passes you. When the car is coming towards you, the gaps between the waves are
smaller, but when the car passes you, you are suddenly behind it, so the gaps in
the waves quickly begin increasing. You can hear this quick change in the video
below. As the F1 car passes very quickly, you go from being in front of it to
behind it very fast so the difference in pitch of the car is very noticable
